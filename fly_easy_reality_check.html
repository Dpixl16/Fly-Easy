The first set is what to ask the people on your outreach list, sharpened enough that a polite "looks great!" won't satisfy it. The second is what I'd ask you, if I were being fully honest instead of just encouraging.

Questions to ask them Questions I have for you

Part one — questions to ask them

Grouped the same way as your outreach list, so you can pull the right ones into each conversation. Each is built from something specific already on the site — not a hypothetical.

01

Aviation professionals

"Read the in-flight tips out loud — is there anything here you'd never actually say to a fellow pilot, or that sounds like it came from a generic travel-blog list instead of someone who flies?"

Why this one: forces a line-by-line reaction instead of a general impression — pointed at specific lines like "sit behind the wing" and "watch takeoff and landing to calm your nerves."

"If you were grading the SFO terminal write-ups on accuracy today, what's the one fact you'd bet is already out of date?"

Why this one: your own changelog shows terminal and airline assignments have needed correcting more than once — this question assumes something is wrong right now, rather than asking if it might be.

02

Expert & frequent travelers

"The site says arrive 2 hours early for domestic, 3 for international. Is that genuinely useful in 2026, or the same safe, generic number every travel site prints?"

Why this one: tests whether your flagship timing advice is actually differentiated, or interchangeable with any airline's own FAQ page.

"Would you actually open Timeblocker or Packer before a real trip — or just do the math and packing in your head like you always have? If the second one, what's missing that would change that?"

Why this one: separates "neat tool" from "tool I'd actually use," which is the only distinction that matters for a frequent traveler.

03

Underrepresented segments

"If you were flying through SFO tomorrow, what's the first accessibility question this site fails to answer — and does that absence feel like an oversight, or like the site wasn't built with you in mind?"

Why this one: doesn't ask "should we add accessibility content" (easy to say yes to) — it asks how the current gap actually feels, which is harder to hear and more useful.

"Read this line: 'watch takeoff and landing — one of the simplest ways to calm pre-flight nerves.' Does that land as real advice, or as something written by someone who's never actually been afraid to fly?"

Why this one: puts an actual line from the site in front of the one person qualified to say whether it's genuinely reassuring or just sounds like it should be.

04

Design & product feedback

"You land on the homepage with zero context. In ten seconds, do you know what this is for, why you'd trust its advice, and what to click first — or are you guessing?"

Why this one: a real first-visit test, timed, instead of "what do you think of the homepage" after they've already read the whole thing.

"This is really four separate tools sharing a nav bar — Global Tips, SFO Tips, Timeblocker, Packer. Does it feel like one coherent product, or four features that happen to live at the same URL?"

Why this one: gets at structure, not surface polish — the kind of feedback that's uncomfortable because fixing it means more than a color change.

05

Outside the box

"Of everything you hear passengers complain about on the way to the airport, what's the complaint that never seems to get fixed no matter how many articles get written about it?"

Why this one: a rideshare driver has heard the real, unfiltered version of airport stress hundreds of times — this asks for the pattern, not a one-off anecdote.

"Why should a stranger trust specific timing and gate advice from a site with no name, bio, or credentials attached to it, over their airline's own app?"

Why this one: a fair, pointed question for a journalist to ask on the record — and one worth sitting with yourself. See the next section.

Part two — questions I have for you

You asked me to be honest and make these hard. So — no hedging, in the order they occurred to me while going through the actual code.

01

Why would a stranger trust specific numeric advice — arrive 2 hours early, this museum is in Terminal 1 — from a site that doesn't say who's behind it or how they know?

There's no About page and no author name anywhere — just "an independent guide, not affiliated with any airport or airline" in the footer. That's honest, but it's also the only credibility signal on the entire site. Is the anonymity deliberate, or something that just never got addressed?

02

Your own changelog shows you've already had to correct real errors more than once — a retired boarding process, wrong terminal assignments, a stale exhibit date, wrong CDC guidance. All of it hand-entered, with no "last verified" date shown anywhere in the UI. What's the actual plan for catching the next wrong fact before a reader relies on it, given you're the only one checking?

This isn't hypothetical — it's happened multiple times already, which means it's a pattern, not a fluke. A solo maintainer with no freshness signal to readers is a real structural risk for a site whose whole premise is trustworthy, accurate tips.

03

You want to expand to 2-3 more airports. Given that SFO alone has needed several rounds of correction, is more airports the right next move — or does it risk diluting the one thing that's actually your edge over a generic travel site: deep, accurate SFO knowledge?

Breadth and depth compete for the same limited time. Worth being honest about which one you're actually optimizing for before committing to both.

04

If you stripped out the photography and the golden-hour styling, would "beauty of aviation" still be present in the actual substance of the site — or is it mostly a visual wrapper around what's functionally a logistics and stress-management tool?

Timeblocker, Packer, and the terminal explorer are all pure utility. The beauty pillar lives almost entirely in the design layer right now, not in the content or features themselves. That's not necessarily wrong — but it's worth knowing which pillar is actually load-bearing.

05

Your audience list runs from pilots and aviation professionals to a first-time nervous flyer traveling with kids. If you had to cut that list down to the one person you're building for first, who survives — and would the aviation-enthusiast content and the anxious-flyer content still feel like they belong on the same site if you did?

A broad audience is comfortable to write down and uncomfortable to actually design for — those two readers want almost opposite tones. Right now the site speaks in one voice to both.

06

You're lining up expert feedback before the site has gone out to much real traffic yet. Is the honest bottleneck right now polish and accuracy — or is it that almost nobody outside the people you've directly asked has actually used the site?

Expert feedback and user feedback answer different questions. If the real gap is traffic, then the sharpest experts in the world won't tell you what actual travelers hit and bounce off.

07

This is a solo, spare-time, static site giving people specific advice about missing flights and security timing. If someone plans a trip around "2 hours is enough" and it isn't — a holiday, a bad TSA day — how much of that risk is really covered by the small-print disclaimer, versus something you'd personally feel responsible for?

Not meant to be alarmist — the footer disclaimer is reasonable and standard. But it's worth actually sitting with the gap between "legally covered" and "how it would feel" before treating it as fully settled.

One honest note on receiving the answers: the useful reaction to a hard answer is writing it down before you respond to it — not explaining the context, not defending the decision. You can decide what to do with it later; the value is in hearing it uncut first.
