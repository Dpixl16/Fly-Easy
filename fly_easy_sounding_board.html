Fly Easy — Feedback & Outreach

Who to ask next

A working list of people and organizations who could give Fly Easy honest, specific feedback — on the website itself, on general aviation tips, and on SFO-specific accuracy. Tap a status pill as you reach out; it's saved locally in this browser so the list stays useful as you work through it.

Already in motion: you've reached out to contacts at Oakland (OAK) and San Francisco (SFO) airports and are waiting to hear back. That's a good instinct, but both are large, PR-cautious organizations that get a lot of inbound requests — a slow or no reply is normal, not a signal to stop. Two backups worth trying in parallel rather than after: San Jose Mineta (SJC) — a smaller Bay Area airport, likely less deluged with requests — and, at OAK/SFO specifically, their community relations or guest-experience teams if you originally wrote to operations (or vice versa), since it's a different inbox and a different person's job to answer exactly this kind of question.

01

Aviation professionals

People who can fact-check the flying-specific content and catch anything that reads as slightly off to someone who actually does this for a living.

General-aviation pilots

Why

They fly for the love of it, so "beauty of aviation" is their native language — and they'll immediately spot a tip (turbulence, seating, timing) that's slightly wrong.

Where

EAA Chapter 20 at San Carlos Airport (KSQL) — a real, active hobbyist-pilot chapter about 20 minutes from SFO. Their meetings are open to visitors.

Flight attendants

Why

They watch traveler stress up close, all day, and can confirm or correct the in-flight comfort tips in Global Tips from direct experience.

Where

r/flightattendants, or simply ask one directly next time you fly — most are happy to talk shop on a quiet flight.

Air traffic controllers / dispatchers

Why

Could validate anything touching real-time flight/gate info, and would have an informed opinion on ideas like a live-ATC-audio feature.

Where

Local NATCA chapter, or aviation forums where controllers post off-duty.

A smaller airport's guest-experience team

Why

SJC is a real backup to OAK/SFO — smaller, likely more responsive, and useful context for your own plan to expand terminal-explorer coverage beyond SFO.

Where

San Jose Mineta International Airport's community/guest-relations contact.

Retired airline or airport employees

Why

Deep institutional knowledge with none of a current employee's PR caution — and often genuinely enjoy being asked.

Where

LinkedIn search for "retired" + gate agent / ramp / airport operations; local retiree or aviation-history groups.

02

Expert & frequent travelers

People who've flown enough to have strong, specific opinions — exactly the bluntness you're asking for.

FlyerTalk forums

Why

The most detail-obsessed frequent-flyer community online — veteran travelers who will bluntly flag anything inaccurate or outdated, not just say "looks nice."

Where

Post in a relevant subforum (travel tools, or a regional SFO/Bay Area thread) with specific questions, not a general "check out my site."

Mainstream travel subreddits

Why

A useful contrast to FlyerTalk's expert lens — more like your "occasional traveler" audience segment, less specialist, more representative.

Where

r/travel, r/awardtravel, or Bay Area–specific subreddits.

Independent aviation bloggers/YouTubers

Why

They review travel tools and sites as part of their work, so their feedback tends to be structured and public — and even a passing mention is exposure to exactly your target audience.

Where

Aviation-focused channels and blogs (not just points/miles-focused ones) — look for creators who cover the airport experience itself, not just award bookings.

A local travel advisor/agent

Why

Hears live client complaints about airports every week — grounded in what ordinary (non-enthusiast) travelers actually struggle with, day to day.

Where

A local travel agency, or a travel-advisor professional association chapter.

03

Underrepresented segments

The feedback most likely to surface something you can't see from the inside — because the gap is in whose experience the site currently reflects.

Accessible-travel advocates

Why

Fly Easy currently has no accessibility content at all (TSA Cares, the Hidden Disabilities Sunflower program, etc.) — someone who navigates this daily would give you the single most valuable feedback on this list, precisely because it's an invisible gap to everyone else.

Where

Wheelchair Travel (John Morris) or similar accessible-travel writers and communities.

A fear-of-flying course provider

Why

Their entire profession is reducing flight anxiety — they'd know precisely which of your reassurances actually land versus which read as generic.

Where

Programs like SOAR or British Airways' Flying With Confidence — worth an email even as a "would you take a look" ask.

Family/parent travel bloggers

Why

Tests whether the family-traveler content solves real friction (kids' spots, family security lanes) rather than assumed friction.

Where

Family-travel blogs, or a local parents' Facebook/Nextdoor group.

International students / non-native English speakers

Why

SFO's international terminals serve non-native English speakers constantly — whether your tips actually read clearly to them is a blind spot most English-first sites never check.

Where

A local university's international student office or student group.

A genuinely nervous flier

Why

Not a professional — just someone who gets anxious flying. The most direct test of whether the stress-reduction framing actually reduces anxiety, instead of just your own guess at what would.

Where

Likely someone you already know — worth asking directly rather than a stranger.

04

Design & product feedback

For the website as a piece of software — navigation, clarity, whether the tools actually work the way people expect.

Indie Hackers community

Why

Builders used to giving specific, structured critique on real live products — not vague praise, and not shy about pointing out what's confusing.

Where

A "Show IH" style post on indiehackers.com describing what Fly Easy is and what feedback you want.

A Bay Area UX/product meetup

Why

In-person critique from people who do this professionally, with the benefit of being local enough to attend easily.

Where

Meetup.com groups for UX/product design in San Francisco.

A local UX bootcamp instructor

Why

Bootcamp students often need a real, live site for portfolio-critique assignments — you could get several detailed reviews for free.

Where

General Assembly SF, Springboard, or a local community college UX program.

05

Outside the box

People who aren't obviously "feedback sources" but who are closer to real, unfiltered airport stress than almost anyone else on this list.

Rideshare/taxi drivers doing SFO runs

Why

They hear real, unfiltered traveler stress in the back seat constantly — completely candid, and free to ask next time you take a ride.

Where

Your next Uber/Lyft/taxi to or from SFO — just ask.

EAA Chapter 20, San Carlos Airport

Why

A real, active local hobbyist-pilot community — a low-pressure way to get a whole room of aviation-obsessed opinions at once, and a genuine gut-check on whether the site's "beauty of aviation" angle lands with people who live it.

Where

eaa20.org — chapter meetings are typically open to visitors.

A local journalist or blogger

Why

"One person's project to fix airport stress" is a genuinely pitchable human-interest angle — and journalists ask sharp, skeptical questions by profession, so even an interview that goes nowhere tends to surface your weakest points.

Where

A Bay Area local-news or travel section reporter.

How to ask, so you actually get useful answers

  • Be specific, not general. "What do you think of my website" gets a polite "looks good." "Does this Timeblocker step order match what you'd actually do at SFO Terminal 2?" gets a real answer.
  • Point them at one page, not the whole site. Feedback on everything at once tends to average out into nothing useful — pick the page most relevant to who you're asking.
  • Make responding effortless. A direct question they can answer in one reply beats a link to fill out the full feedback form, especially for a first contact.
  • Follow up once, then move on. A single polite follow-up after a week or two is normal; beyond that, put the energy into the next name on this list instead.
